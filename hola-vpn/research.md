# Research: Hola VPN - VPN Service - CPI (US) {Desktop Only}, Native banner

## 1. Offer check
- **Offer:** Hola VPN, a freemium VPN / proxy app. The free version runs on a community (peer-to-peer) network,
  so free users' devices share idle resources and carry other users' traffic. The paid plan (Hola VPN Plus)
  uses dedicated servers. The main user benefit is choosing which country your browsing appears to come
  from, so you can reach sites and pages that show "not available in your region".
- **Conversion:** CPI, which pays when the desktop app is installed (Windows / Mac). **US only, desktop only:**
  target desktop devices in every ad set; mobile clicks won't convert.
- **Payout:** $3 per install (given as "$3 install"). Shown in the EPC chip as given; information only.
- **Slug:** `hola-vpn`
- **Vertical:** Other (consumer software / VPN utility). No special guardrails apply, but the general rules
  still do: no invented proof and no scare claims.
- **Affiliate link:** `https://afflat3e3.com/trk/lnk/6BED165C-0A6B-4396-97AF-CF7A9457314A/?o=31563&c=918277&a=781113&k=50A459484F5FFA5FEC225C4B88834397&l=37878`
  (it already has a `?`, so each page adds `&sub=<angle>`). Both the tracker domain and hola.org were blocked
  from this build environment, so neither the offer page nor Hola's own claims could be checked. Open the
  link on a desktop before spending.
- **Traffic:** Native banner (native ad networks: Taboola, Outbrain, Adsterra Native Banner, MGID and
  similar). This overrides the Facebook + Native default.
- **Test budget:** $50 split across 3 angles (~$16.67 each), one campaign or ad set per angle.

### Native network policy check: allowed, with restrictions
VPN and utility software are a normal, allowed vertical on native and display networks. Adsterra lists
VPN and utilities among its main verticals. What the networks restrict:
- **No unexpected downloads (Taboola Prohibited Campaign Practices):** no content that causes the download
  of software "resulting in an unexpected experience". The landers never start a download. Every CTA says
  plainly that it leads to the app's page for a Windows or Mac computer.
- **No fake system or security alerts:** no "your computer is infected / your IP is exposed" warnings,
  browser-style pop-ups or fake scan results. None are used.
- **Clear advertising label:** "Advertisement · Advertorial" at the top of each advertorial, plus the
  native line in the footer.
- **Accurate claims:** independent reviews say the free tier has no encryption to speak of and routes
  traffic through other users' devices. So the pages **do not** claim security, encryption, anonymity or
  "protect your data". They talk only about access and choosing a country. The terms and footer add a line
  that a VPN does not make you anonymous and some sites don't allow VPN access.
- **Streaming:** the pages don't name streaming services and don't promise to "unblock" any specific one.
  Some services forbid VPN access in their terms.

## 2. Avatar
1. **Who:** US adults, 25–65, on a home or work desktop / laptop. Middle income. Mix of frequent travelers,
   people with family or ties abroad, expats returning home, remote workers, and curious everyday web users.
   Daily frustration: the web looks different depending on where you are, and sites say no without
   explaining why.
2. **Top 10 pain points:**
   1. "This content is not available in your country / region" messages.
   2. Their home sites and accounts behave differently or block them while they travel abroad.
   3. Sites from another country (news, shops, family's local services) that won't load from the US.
   4. Video and audio libraries that differ by country, with no say in which one they get.
   5. Not knowing how to "change location" without a technical setup.
   6. VPN products that feel complicated: protocols, servers, settings.
   7. Paying monthly for something they need once in a while.
   8. Free tools that are browser-only or limited, and they want a desktop app.
   9. Prices and pages that look different by region, with no easy way to check.
   10. Feeling the internet decides what they can see based on an IP address.
3. **Already tried:** web proxy sites (slow and full of ads), changing the site's country in settings
   (often ignored), browser extensions that stop working, waiting until they're home, asking a relative
   abroad, big subscription VPNs they found too pricey or complex for occasional use.
4. **What makes them click now:** they just hit a region block (or remember the last one). A plain-English
   explanation of why it happens, plus a simple free app where you pick a country, reads as the way out.
   Low effort and no cost to try.
5. **Where they spend time online:** news and entertainment sites that carry native widgets (Taboola /
   Outbrain recommendations, Adsterra native banners), Reddit (r/VPN, r/travel, r/expats), YouTube "how to
   watch from abroad" videos, travel forums, Facebook groups for expats and travelers.

**Competitor notes:** VPN native ads often use advertorials ("Why everyone is installing this on their
computer") and scare framing about hackers on public Wi-Fi. Many of those scare claims are outdated (most
sites now use HTTPS) and native networks reject fake security alerts. An honest "access, not security"
framing fits this product better and stands out.

**Market context (planning only, not used as claims on the pages):**
- A 2026 third-party review (Gizmodo) reports more than 260 million downloads. Not published by the offer
  owner as far as could be checked (hola.org was unreachable from here), so not used.
- Trustpilot shows about 1,200 reviews with mixed sentiment. Not used.
- Independent reviewers (security.org, top10vpn, vpntesting) criticize the free P2P model on privacy.
  This is why the pages avoid security claims.

## 3. Angle scores (for a US desktop reader on a news or entertainment page)
| Angle | Score | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pain Point | 5 | "Not available in your region" is a real, common, annoying moment, and the fix (pick a country) answers it directly. |
| Curiosity | 4 | "The web you see depends on where it thinks you are" is a factual open loop that native readers click to close. |
| Direct Benefit | 4 | A simple end result: a free desktop app, click a country flag, reach the page. Clean and believable. |
| Social Proof | 2 | No numbers or reviews published by the offer owner could be verified; third-party figures are mixed or unverifiable. Guardrail: no proof → skip. |
| Fear | 1 | The usual VPN fear (hackers, exposed data) would be a security claim this free P2P product can't honestly back, and native networks reject scare alerts. Skip. |

**Picked:** Pain Point (5), Curiosity (4), Direct Benefit (4).
**Skipped:** Social Proof (no verifiable proof from the offer owner), Fear (would require security claims
the product can't support, and it reads as a fake alert).

## Format per page
All three pages are **Advertorials** (one format for the whole test, so the result isn't muddied by format):
- **Pain Point → Advertorial:** native readers expect an article next to articles, and a pain story
  reads best as an editorial piece.
- **Curiosity → Advertorial:** native traffic is cold and reading-minded. A quiz suits social traffic and
  a location-free desktop install doesn't need qualifying questions, so the "why the web looks different"
  explainer is told as an article.
- **Direct Benefit → Advertorial:** Direct pages suit search traffic that already knows what it wants.
  Native readers need a short presell first, so the result-led copy goes into an advertorial with a CTA in the hero.
